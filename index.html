Food nutrition scorecard

216 everyday foods and drinks in nine groups, each rated 0–10 against its own group on what matters for that group. Compare them per 100 g (3.5 oz), which is fair by weight, per serving, the amount people usually eat, or per 100 calories, which shows what each food gives for its calories. Fruits and vegetables are raw; meat, fish, grains and beans are cooked; the rest are as sold.

Hover or tap any square for the real amount, or a food’s name for its serving and what it’s best at. Tap a heading to sort.

Search and compare

Find any food or drink and tap it to compare, up to three side by side. Amounts follow the switch at the top (per 100 g, per serving or per 100 calories); the best on each line is highlighted (lowest for sugar, saturated fat and sodium).

    Vitamins

    Vitamin C

    Supports your immune system, helps make collagen for skin, gums and wound healing, works as an antioxidant, and helps you absorb iron from plant foods. Citrus fruit and their juices, peppers, kiwi and berries are rich sources.

    Vitamin A

    Keeps your eyes working well, especially in dim light, supports immunity, and keeps skin and the linings of your body healthy. Liver is so rich in it that eating liver more than about once a week can give too much, especially in pregnancy.

    Helps you absorb calcium and keep bones strong, and supports muscles and the immune system. Few foods have much; in many countries milk is fortified with it, and sunlight on skin makes the rest.

    Low sugar

    Whole fruit and vegetables pack their sugar with fiber and water, so they suit most people. Juice and soda don’t, which is why sugary drinks are the biggest source of added sugar for many people. Scored for fruits, vegetables, grains, sweets and drinks.

    —

    Low sodium

    Too much sodium raises blood pressure for many people. Fresh foods are naturally low; cured meats, cheese, bread, instant noodles and canned beans can be high. On the meat chart, 0 means the amount gives 20% or more of the daily limit.

    —
    —
    —
    —

    Low saturated fat

    Diets high in saturated fat raise LDL cholesterol. Fish, skinless poultry, low-fat milk and most vegetable oils are low; fatty meats, butter, ghee, lard, cream, cheese, and coconut and palm oil are high. On the meat chart, 0 means the amount gives 20% or more of the daily limit.

    —
    —
    —

    Low trans fat new

    The most harmful fat for your heart: it raises LDL and lowers HDL cholesterol. Partially hydrogenated oils, used in some shortenings and hard margarines, were the main source, and many countries now limit or ban them; butter, ghee and meat have small natural amounts. Scored for oils and fats.

    —

    Low calories

    Drinks differ a lot in energy: water, tea, coffee and diet sodas have almost none, while juices, sodas and hot cocoa carry as much as a snack, without filling you up. Scored for drinks.

    —

    Also worth knowing (not scored)

    Plant omega-3 (ALA) not scored

    The plant form of omega-3. Your body turns only a little of it into the EPA and DHA found in fish, but ALA itself is linked to heart health. Not scored because the USDA file used here doesn’t list it, so walnuts, chia and canola oil get no credit for it in the charts.

    Found in: flaxseed and its oil, chia seeds, walnuts, canola and soybean oil

    Lycopene not scored

    A red pigment that works as an antioxidant and is linked to heart health. Not scored because most fruits and vegetables have none; cooking tomatoes makes it easier to absorb.

    Found in: tomatoes, tomato juice, watermelon, guava, papaya and pink grapefruit

    Nitrates not scored

    Natural nitrates in vegetables turn into nitric oxide in your body, which relaxes blood vessels. They can lower blood pressure slightly and help exercise endurance. Not scored because amounts swing widely with soil and season.

    Found in: beets and beetroot juice, spinach, lettuce, celery and other leafy greens

    Sulforaphane not scored

    Cabbage-family vegetables hold compounds that turn into sulforaphane when chopped or chewed. It switches on the body’s own antioxidant and cleanup defenses and is being studied for cancer prevention. Not scored because USDA doesn’t measure it.

    Found in: broccoli, Brussels sprouts, kale, collard greens, cabbage and cauliflower

    Live cultures (probiotics) not scored

    Yogurt, buttermilk and fermented foods like natto and tempeh can carry live bacteria that may support gut health. Not scored because amounts depend on the brand and how it was made.

    Found in: yogurt, kefir, natto, tempeh and other fermented foods

    How the scores work, and where the numbers come from
    • Each chart rates its foods 0–10 against the others in the same group, only on what matters for that group (a column is left out when most foods in the group have none of it). The food with the most gets 10 and the rest follow a square-root scale, so a food with a quarter as much still scores 5 and one standout, like raisins for antioxidants, can’t push everything else down to 0. Any food giving at least 5% of the daily value scores at least 1.
    • Lower-is-better columns (sugar, sodium, fats, calories) run from the best in the group (10) to the worst (0). On the meat chart, sodium and saturated fat use a fixed scale instead: 0 means 20% or more of the daily limit.
    • Overall is the plain average of a food’s scores in its chart. It rewards all-rounders; a low overall doesn’t make a food unhealthy. Because scores are relative, the small line under each heading shows how much the top food actually gives. Key nutrients only hides columns; overall still uses them all.
    • Per 100 g compares equal weights, so dry foods like nuts, raisins and popcorn score high. Per serving uses a typical portion, shown in each food’s pop-up. Per 100 calories shows how much nutrition each food gives for its calories; water, plain tea and coffee and diet cola have almost no calories, so they show a dash there.
    • In Build your mix, Tap to pick scores each food on 26 nutrients by how much of a day’s needs it gives (10 means half the daily value or more). Portions adds up real servings as a share of the daily value. Where there is no official daily value, a common target is used: 0.5 g omega-3, 6 mg lutein and 1,000 mg polyphenols.
    • Nutrients come from USDA’s Standard Reference database (SR Legacy); a missing value counts as 0. Ugali is an estimate (white maize flour cooked stiff with water), beetroot juice comes from a brand’s label, and a few brand names (KitKat, M&M’s, Kellogg’s corn flakes, Powerade, Red Bull) stand in for their type.
    • Antioxidants are total polyphenols from the Phenol-Explorer database: its list of the 100 richest foods, plus a broader lab test for 38 more foods, such as banana, kale and apple juice. That test also picks up some vitamin C and sugar, so treat those values as rough; each pop-up says which source a value uses. A 0 means not measured or very low.
    • Trans fat is from USDA; oils with no figure (olive, sunflower, palm and others) count as 0, and ghee’s is estimated from butter.
